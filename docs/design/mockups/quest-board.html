Quest board looks

Style Q4: what the player watches while a party is out (D-004). The same moment in every option: Wenna's party, four strong, deep in the Hollow Barrow on day 2. Sefa is down, three barrow-wights are up, and the calendar has stopped for a major key moment. The highlights log on the right is the same in all four; only the board changes.

A. Painted tiles and minis

StrengthsReads like a dungeon board game: rooms appear as the party explores, minis move square by square. Very clear positions and a strong tabletop feel.
WeaknessesTile art per biome (barrows, caves, forts, forests) adds up. Positions this precise suggest a tactics game the simulation is not (D-004), and players will want to move the minis themselves.

B. Cartographer's map with standees Recommended

StrengthsThe map draws itself in ink as the party explores, with margin notes from the scout, so it is the party's own record and fits the ledger desk. Flat standees with portraits are cheap to make per class and ancestry. Loose positions match a simulation that is not a tactics game. Generated dungeons (D-030) render well as line work. The finished map can go into the Chronicle.
WeaknessesLess spectacle than painted tiles or a diorama; fights read through standee movement, the log and small effects, not animation. Ink line work needs a good procedural renderer to look hand-drawn rather than vector-clean.

C. Isometric diorama

StrengthsThe most cinematic: painted 3D-looking miniatures on terrain, lighting, depth. Best for trailers and the store page.
WeaknessesThe most expensive by far: modelled or rendered minis per class, ancestry and monster, terrain kits per biome, a camera. Closest to the full-3D look D-004 ruled out, and it pulls budget from the management screens that are the actual game.

D. Encounter card spread

StrengthsThe cheapest and the most readable: each encounter is a card turned over, progress is obvious, and it scales to any quest type, including defence and warfare. Very little art per biome.
WeaknessesNo sense of place. "Minis on a map board" (D-004) becomes "cards on a table", so the quest stops feeling like a journey through somewhere. Closer to a card game than to a guild watching its people in a dungeon.

Shared by all four: one party watched live on the board, others as summary cards on the desk (CONCEPT §2); every board movement comes from the same simulation events as the log (GAME-02); the key moment arrives sealed and stops the calendar. Names, places and rolls are placeholders.
